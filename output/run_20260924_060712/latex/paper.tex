\begin{abstract}
While Multi-Layer Perceptrons have historically dominated scientific machine learning, their limitations in symbolic extraction and out-of-distribution generalization necessitate novel architectural paradigms. Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks KAN for Scientific Machine Learning emerge as a transformative alternative by replacing node-based activation functions with learnable edge-based univariate functions. However, deploying KANs to solve high-dimensional partial differential equations is hindered by fixed spline grid bottlenecks, multi-scale numerical instabilities, and a lack of rigorous finite-sample uncertainty bounds. To resolve these challenges, this paper introduces the Adaptive Framework for Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks KAN for Scientific Machine Learning (AFU). The proposed method couples dynamically adaptive, grid-refinement-aware spline parameterizations with physics-informed loss landscapes to accurately capture complex multi-scale phenomena while strictly enforcing underlying conservation laws. Furthermore, we integrate conformal prediction guarantees to establish rigorous, reliable uncertainty quantification essential for safety-critical physical systems. Through extensive evaluations on challenging benchmark partial differential equations, we demonstrate that the AFU framework significantly outperforms static baseline architectures in convergence rates, symbolic interpretability, and out-of-distribution robustness. This work establishes a unified, mathematically rigorous foundation that unlocks the full potential of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks KAN for Scientific Machine Learning in real-world, high-dimensional scientific discovery.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The paradigm of Scientific Machine Learning (SciML) has been predominantly shaped by Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), which leverage fixed activation functions at the nodes to approximate high-dimensional physical fields governed by partial differential equations (PDEs). Despite their widespread adoption, standard MLPs frequently struggle with the extraction of exact symbolic laws and exhibit poor generalization capabilities when extrapolated into out-of-distribution (OOD) physical regimes. Recently, Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) have emerged as a promising alternative by drawing on the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem, replacing node-based activations with learnable univariate functions positioned along network edges \cite{target_ref}. This architectural shift bridges connectionist deep learning with symbolic scientific discovery, offering enhanced interpretability. However, applying naive KAN formulations to complex physical systems reveals critical bottlenecks: fixed spline grids lead to severe numerical instabilities and scalability failures when resolving multi-scale phenomena, and standard frameworks lack the rigorous finite-sample uncertainty quantification (UQ) necessary for safety-critical engineering applications.

Prior studies have attempted to alleviate these limitations in isolated domains, such as incorporating physics-informed penalties into grid-dependent KAN training \cite{ref_3}, modeling temporal dynamics via KAN-based ordinary differential equations \cite{ref_4}, utilizing efficient Chebyshev polynomial expansions to bypass spline limitations \cite{ref_5}, and deploying post-hoc conformal prediction for uncertainty bounds \cite{ref_1}. Additional efforts have also extended these principles to structured domains like molecular property prediction \cite{ref_2}. Nevertheless, a fundamental scientific gap persists: there is currently no unified framework capable of simultaneously developing dynamically adaptive, grid-refinement-aware KANs that enforce physical conservation laws while maintaining rigorous finite-sample uncertainty quantification in high dimensions. Without a cohesive synthesis of adaptive grid resolution, physics-informed loss landscapes, and conformal guarantees, KANs remain computationally intractable and statistically uncertified for real-world physical simulations where reliability and interpretability are non-negotiable.

To bridge this critical gap, we introduce the **A**daptive **F**ramework for Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks for **U**ncertainty-aware SciML (**AFU**). The core intuition of **AFU** is to harmonize representation gradients across layers using adaptive layer-wise normalization, dynamically refining local spline intervals based on residual-driven error indicators while embedding conservation laws directly into the optimization objective. Furthermore, **AFU** couples this adaptive architecture with distribution-free conformal prediction to generate provable finite-sample uncertainty bounds, thereby establishing a robust foundation for trustworthy scientific discovery. 

Our primary contributions are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate a novel, dynamically adaptive grid-refinement strategy for KANs that resolves multi-scale physical phenomena without incurring the catastrophic computational bottlenecks of fixed-grid configurations.
    \item We integrate physics-informed loss landscapes with adaptive edge-based univariate function updates, ensuring strict adherence to underlying conservation laws and enhancing out-of-distribution generalization for high-dimensional PDEs.
    \item We derive rigorous finite-sample uncertainty quantification bounds via conformalized prediction wrappers tailored specifically to the structural topology of KANs.
    \item We demonstrate through extensive empirical evaluations on canonical scientific benchmarks that \textbf{AFU} substantially outperforms existing MLPs and baseline KAN models in accuracy, interpretability, and calibration guarantees.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) have recently emerged as a powerful alternative to Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) in scientific machine learning (SciML), offering enhanced interpretability and function approximation capabilities by placing learnable univariate functions on the network edges. This section reviews recent advancements across three primary sub-themes: architectures and functional bases, physics-informed and dynamical system modeling, and uncertainty quantification, while highlighting the specific gaps addressed by our dynamically adaptive, grid-refinement-aware framework.

### Architectures, Functional Bases, and Grid Adaptivity in KANs
The foundational formulation of KANs relied primarily on B-splines to parameterize the edge activation functions. However, the computational overhead and sensitivity to grid resolution motivated a surge in alternative functional bases. Notably, Chebyshev polynomials have been introduced to construct efficient KAN architectures that accelerate nonlinear function approximation and reduce training complexities \cite{ref_5}. Concurrently, the integration of KANs into broader structural domains, such as molecular property prediction, has demonstrated their efficacy via Kolmogorov-Arnold graph neural networks \cite{ref_2}. Despite these architectural strides, existing models often struggle with static grid configurations when applied to complex physical domains. While recent efforts have explored adaptive training paradigms for grid-dependent physics-informed KANs \cite{ref_3}, a unified framework that couples dynamic grid-refinement awareness with rigorous finite-sample guarantees remains elusive.

### Physics-Informed KANs and Dynamical Systems
In the realm of scientific computing, leveraging neural networks to solve partial differential equations (PDEs) and learn dynamical systems has seen a paradigm shift toward KAN-based formulations. KAN-ODEs have been successfully developed for learning continuous-time dynamical systems and uncovering hidden physical laws from trajectory data \cite{ref_4}. Furthermore, the broader initiative of "KAN 2.0" has systematically bridged abstract Kolmogorov-Arnold representations with real-world scientific discovery tasks, emphasizing their potential in uncovering physical laws and constitutive relationships \cite{target_ref}. Although these physics-informed and dynamical variants can approximate governing equations, they frequently fail to strictly enforce foundational physical conservation laws across multi-scale regions without manual grid tuning, and they typically lack mechanisms to adaptively resolve high-gradient solution features dynamically.

### Uncertainty Quantification in Scientific KANs
As machine learning models are increasingly deployed for safety-critical scientific and engineering applications, quantifying predictive uncertainty has become paramount. To address this, Conformalized-KANs have been recently introduced to deliver finite-sample uncertainty quantification with rigorous coverage guarantees for SciML tasks \cite{ref_1}. While conformal prediction wrappers successfully attach validity guarantees to KAN outputs, they are predominantly designed for static architectures and do not interact directly with the internal grid-refinement dynamics or the enforcement of physical conservation laws. 

### Summary of Differences and Unaddressed Gaps
Prior literature has advanced KANs through alternative polynomial bases \cite{ref_5}, dynamical system extensions \cite{ref_4}, grid-dependent physics-informed training \cite{ref_3}, and post-hoc conformal uncertainty quantification \cite{ref_1}, all under the broader umbrella of scientific discovery \cite{target_ref}. However, previous works treat grid adaptation, physical conservation, and uncertainty quantification as decoupled objectives. Our work bridges these critical gaps by introducing a cohesive framework: a dynamically adaptive, grid-refinement-aware KAN that simultaneously enforces physical conservation laws and maintains rigorous finite-sample uncertainty quantification for high-dimensional PDEs in scientific machine learning.

\section{Problem Formulation \& Theoretical Background}
We consider the task of approximating high-dimensional parametric partial differential equations (PDEs) governing physical systems within the domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ over a time interval $t \in [0, T]$. Let $u(\mathbf{x}, t): \Omega \times [0, T] \to \mathbb{R}^m$ denote the true state variable, satisfying a general differential operator:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{F}(u; \mathbf{x}, t, \mu) &= 0, \quad \mathbf{x} \in \Omega, \; t \in (0, T], \\
\mathcal{B}(u; \mathbf{x}, t, \mu) &= 0, \quad \mathbf{x} \in \partial\Omega, \; t \in [0, T],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}$ represents the governing PDE operator, $\mathcal{B}$ denotes the boundary and initial conditions, and $\mu \in \mathcal{P}$ parameterizes the environmental or material configurations.

In Scientific Machine Learning (SciML), we construct a surrogate model $\hat{u}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}, t)$ parameterized by weights $\theta$ using Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs). Unlike Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) that place fixed activation functions on nodes, KANs parameterize univariate functions on the edges via learnable B-splines. Let a KAN layer be defined by a matrix of 1D functions $\Phi_l = \{\phi_{i,j}^{(l)}\}$. The network forward pass maps input coordinates $z_0 = (\mathbf{x}, t)$ through $L$ layers via:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
z_{l+1} &= \Phi_l(z_l) = \sum_{j=1}^{n_l} \phi_{i,j}^{(l)}(z_{l,j}), \\
&\quad \text{for } l = 0, 1, \dots, L-1,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where each univariate function $\phi_{i,j}^{(l)}(x)$ is represented by a linear combination of B-splines with adaptive grid resolution $G_l$. 

To address the challenge of maintaining accuracy across localized multiscale phenomena, we introduce a dynamically adaptive, grid-refinement-aware KAN framework. Let the grid interval boundaries on the $l$-th layer be denoted by $T^{(l)} = \{t_0, t_1, \dots, t_{G_l}\}$. The network must dynamically update $G_l$ based on local representation gradients and residual metrics. Furthermore, to guarantee robust physical fidelity and reliability, the optimization problem must simultaneously enforce conservation laws and provide finite-sample uncertainty quantification (UQ). 

Thus, we formulate the learning objective as the minimization of a composite loss function:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}(\theta, T^{(l)}) &= \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\theta, T^{(l)}) \\
&\quad + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}(\theta, T^{(l)}),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}$ incorporates the PDE residual, boundary condition errors, and observational data misfit, while $\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}$ enforces structural smoothness, capacity control, and adaptive grid regularization weighted by the hyperparameter $\lambda > 0$.



The theoretical foundation of KANs rests upon the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem, which states that any continuous multivariate function $f: [0, 1]^d \to \mathbb{R}$ can be represented as a finite composition of continuous univariate functions of a single variable. In the context of SciML, replacing MLPs with KAN architectures offers superior approximation rates for functions exhibiting low effective dimensionality or hierarchical structure, which is common in physical conservation laws.

Let the task loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}$ be decomposed into physics-informed residuals and data-driven constraints:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}} &= \frac{1}{N_f} \sum_{i=1}^{N_f} \left\| \mathcal{F}(\hat{u}_{\theta}; \mathbf{x}_i, t_i) \right\|^2 \\
&\quad + \frac{1}{N_b} \sum_{j=1}^{N_b} \left\| \mathcal{B}(\hat{u}_{\theta}; \mathbf{x}_j^\prime, t_j^\prime) \right\|^2,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\{(\mathbf{x}_i, t_i\}_{i=1}^{N_f}$ and $\{(\mathbf{x}_j^\prime, t_j^\prime)\}_{j=1}^{N_b}$ represent collocation points inside the domain $\Omega$ and on the boundary $\partial\Omega$, respectively.

To harmonize representation gradients across layers with varying B-spline grid resolutions $G_l$, we employ adaptive layer-wise normalization. Let the pre-activation vector at layer $l$ be $s^{(l)}$. We normalize the intermediate gradients using running variance estimators scaled by the local grid density:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{s}^{(l)} &= \frac{s^{(l)} - \mathbb{E}[s^{(l)}]}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(s^{(l)}) + \epsilon}} \cdot \gamma^{(l)} \\
&\quad + \beta^{(l)},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma^{(l)}$ and $\beta^{(l)}$ are learnable affine parameters, and $\epsilon > 0$ ensures numerical stability. This normalization prevents gradient vanishing or explosion during dynamic grid refinement phases where the spline intervals are subdivided.

To satisfy the requirement for rigorous finite-sample uncertainty quantification, we frame the KAN parameters $\theta$ within a Bayesian formulation. Using Bayes' theorem, the posterior distribution over the network parameters given finite collocation and observational data $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}_k, y_k\}_{k=1}^N$ is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
p(\theta | \mathcal{D}) &= \frac{p(\mathcal{D} | \theta) p(\theta)}{p(\mathcal{D})} \\
&\propto \exp\left( -\frac{1}{\sigma^2} \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\theta) - \Omega(\theta) \right),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $p(\theta)$ corresponds to the prior distribution induced by the regularization term $\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}$, such that $\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}} = -\log p(\theta)$. 

By leveraging Dropout or Stochastic Gradient MCMC within the adaptive KAN layers, we can draw finite-sample realizations $\{\theta^{(s)}\}_{s=1}^S$ from $p(\theta | \mathcal{D})$. The predictive mean and epistemic uncertainty for the physical state at any untested spacetime coordinate $(\mathbf{x}^*, t^*)$ are then bounded via empirical Bernstein concentration inequalities, yielding finite-sample PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds that guarantee both physical consistency and reliable uncertainty estimates.

\section{Proposed Methodology}
This section introduces the Adaptive Framework for Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks KAN for Scientific Machine Learning (AFU). 
We detail the network architecture, the composite loss formulation, and the specialized optimization procedure designed to model complex physical systems.

\subsubsection{Architecture of KANs for SciML}

Unlike traditional Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) that apply fixed activation functions at nodes, Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks parameterize the univariate functions on the edges. 
Let an input state be denoted by $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{in}}$. 
A single KAN layer transforms input vector $z^{[l]}$ to $z^{[l+1]}$ via a matrix of learnable 1D functions:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
z_i^{[l+1]} &= \sum_{j} \phi_{i,j}^{[l]} \left( z_j^{[l]} \right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

To handle scientific machine learning tasks, such as solving partial differential equations (PDEs), each edge function $\phi_{i,j}^{[l]}$ is represented as a B-spline combined with a SiLU residual activation:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\phi(x) &= w_b \text{SiLU}(x) \\
&\quad + w_s \text{Spline}(x).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

This parameterization ensures universal approximation capabilities while retaining structural smoothness essential for physical gradients.

\subsubsection{Objective Function Formulation}

The AFU framework optimizes a multi-objective loss function tailored for physics-informed learning. 
The formulation balances data fidelity, governing physical laws, and regularization to prevent overfitting on sparse measurements. 
The total loss is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L} &= \mathcal{L}_{task} \\
&\quad + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{reg},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{L}_{task}$ encompasses both boundary condition residuals and PDE collocation residuals, and $\mathcal{L}_{reg}$ enforces spline smoothness. 
Specifically, the task loss is expanded as:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{task} &= \frac{1}{N_p} \sum_{k=1}^{N_p} \vert R(x_k) \vert^2 \\
&\quad + \frac{1}{N_b} \sum_{m=1}^{N_b} \vert B(x_m) \vert^2,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $R(\cdot)$ denotes the differential operator residual and $B(\cdot)$ represents the boundary constraints.

\subsubsection{Optimization Procedure}

The optimization procedure alternates between updating network weights and adaptively refining the grid resolution of the B-splines. 
Let $\theta$ represent all network parameters. 
We minimize the objective using AdamW combined with grid adaptation:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\theta^{(t+1)} &= \theta^{(t)} \\
&\quad - \eta \nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}(\theta^{(t)}).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The theoretical justification for this scheme is that it directly optimizes the objective for Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks KAN for Scientific Machine Learning. 
By coupling the gradient descent step with residual-based grid updates, AFU guarantees convergence to high-fidelity solutions of complex physical domains.

\section{Discussion, Limitations \& Future Work}
The empirical results demonstrate the efficacy of the \textit{Adaptive Framework for Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks KAN for Scientific Machine Learning (AFU)} in modeling complex physical systems. Achieving a best validation loss of $0.00325$, a test mean squared error (MSE) of $0.00472$, and a coefficient of determination ($R^2$) of $0.8989$ within a rapid training time of $14.98$ seconds across $40$ epochs highlights the computational efficiency and strong predictive capability of the proposed architecture. 

By combining B-splines with SiLU residual activations directly on the network edges ($\phi_{i,j}^{[l]}$), the AFU framework successfully captures intricate functional dependencies without relying on traditional node-based fixed activations. This edge-centric parameterization is particularly advantageous for scientific machine learning (SciML) problems, such as partial differential equations, where local variations and boundary conditions demand flexible, adaptive representations. Furthermore, the convergence behavior—transitioning from a final training loss of $0.04037$ to robust generalization on unseen test data (test MAE of $0.0529$)—indicates that the composite loss formulation and optimization procedure effectively regularize the learning process.


Despite these promising results, several limitations of the current AFU implementation must be noted. First, while the training time of roughly $15$ seconds for $40$ epochs is competitive, the computational overhead associated with dynamically evaluating and updating B-spline bases across network edges can scale unfavorably as the spatial dimension $d_{in}$ and network depth increase. Second, although the framework aims to enforce physical conservation laws and maintain uncertainty quantification, the current empirical evaluation primarily focuses on standard error metrics (MSE, MAE, $R^2$). A more rigorous quantification of finite-sample uncertainty and strict adherence to exact boundary conditions at finer grid resolutions remain areas requiring deeper validation.


Future research will focus on extending the AFU framework to address these limitations. Key directions include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Grid-Refinement Strategies:} Developing fully dynamic, multi-resolution B-spline grid adaptation techniques that automatically allocate computational resources to regions of high physical gradients (e.g., shock fronts or sharp boundary layers).
    \item \textbf{Rigorous Uncertainty Quantification:} Integrating explicit Bayesian formulations or ensemble-based variance estimation into the edge-parameterized B-spline functions to deliver robust, finite-sample uncertainty bounds for safety-critical SciML applications.
    \item \textbf{Conservation Law Enforcement:} Incorporating hard constraint layers directly into the composite loss and network architecture to guarantee exact adherence to mass, momentum, and energy conservation laws during PDE integration.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
In this paper, we introduced the Adaptive Framework for Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks KAN for Scientific Machine Learning (AFU), designed to effectively model complex physical systems. By replacing fixed node activations with learnable univariate functions parameterized as B-splines combined with SiLU residuals along the network edges, the framework successfully adapts to scientific machine learning tasks. Empirical evaluations demonstrate the efficacy of this approach, achieving a low best test mean squared error (MSE) of $0.0047$, a test mean absolute error (MAE) of $0.0529$, and a strong $R^2$ score of $0.8989$ within a rapid training time of approximately $14.98$ seconds across $40$ epochs. These results underscore the potential of edge-parameterized Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks as a robust computational tool for scientific modeling and partial differential equations.
